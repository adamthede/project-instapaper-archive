Read the note

The two curves are close to inverses of each other. The years that fill the read archive, 2012 through 2014, left almost nothing behind: seven unread items survive from 2012 against 1,502 finished. The years that fill the backlog, 2016 through 2019, are the years the reading nearly stopped.

2018 is the peak of the queue and it has not moved. The May 2025 export counted 101 unread items saved in 2018; there are 101 today.

The oldest strata behave differently, and the split is exact. All 73 items saved in 2011, 2012 and 2013 sit in folders. Not one of them is in the queue, and the queue holds nothing at all from before 2014. That is two different acts wearing the same label: the queue is where things were dropped, the folders are where things were sorted, named and shelved. The sorted ones are the ones that survived fifteen years unread.

Read the note

The backlog is a technical education he kept buying and never took. Software Development, Ruby on Rails and Web Development together are 3.2 percent of everything he saved and 0.4 percent of everything he read, an almost eight-fold difference that no amount of enrichment asymmetry explains.

What he actually read is broader and softer: Technology, Entrepreneurship, Social Media, Venture Capital. Social Media is 147 mentions in the read corpus and zero in the queue. The pattern is not "he saved hard things and read easy ones" so much as "he saved the things he meant to become, and read the things he was already following".

What this plate cannot show There is no taxonomy of reasons. The model returns one sentence per article, and a sentence about an unread article is an item-level string, so it does not ship. What is publishable is how often it answered and how sure it said it was. The reasons themselves live on the private side of the record.
Read the method

Two fields were deliberately not asked of the model. The abandonment band is arithmetic on read progress, and the topic drift is a corpus computation. Asking a model for either would have produced a plausible number that nothing could check.

The two that were asked, why it was saved and whether it has aged out, are genuinely inferential, and both carry guards. The reason may come back empty, and an empty answer is treated as a result rather than a failure: it happened ten times. The model sets its own confidence, and the 21 rows it marked low are excluded from every aggregate on this page. Aged-out is judged only on rows whose text passed the content check, which is why the denominator is 464 and not 492.

The shortlist is ranked on the enrichment rather than on a fresh model pass, so every part of the ranking traces to a stored field. 382 pieces qualify. The ranked list is published privately, where titles are allowed and the ranking can be judged; the public record gets the count.

Read the note

Hosts ship and article addresses do not. A host is a fact about the archive; a path identifies one article in it, and the whole point of this record is that the articles stay unnamed.

The concentration is the finding. Two hosts are more than a quarter of the queue, and they are also the two hardest to fetch: both bot-block a plain request, which is why the recovery on the cover leans so heavily on the copy Instapaper stored at the moment of saving. The long tail is the other half of the story. 170 hosts contributed exactly one thing he meant to read and never came back to.

Stated exactly as the data supports it This is which leg of the fetch chain produced the text, not whether the URL is still live. The chain short-circuits: the direct fetch runs only where Instapaper held no stored text, so every item Instapaper resolved was never probed against its own URL at all. A live-web survival figure needs a separate probe of all 492 and is not reported here. For scale, a 100-item sample measured while planning this record found 51 URLs still serving their own article. This chain cannot reproduce that number and does not try.
Read the note

The 26 that resolved nowhere are the only items on this page that can be called lost, and even they are not evenly lost. Four were saved in 2011 and seven in 2025: the old ones died of age, the recent ones are paywalls, JavaScript shells and rate limits. Different failure, same outcome.

Two of them were saved as archive links, to services that now rate-limit the request. The hedge against link rot rotted.

The 26 still count as intentions. They are enriched from their title and description alone, flagged as such, and they appear in every year, host and abandonment figure on this page. They simply carry no topics, which is why the topic plate's denominator is smaller than 492.

When this plate becomes readable Two points is a line, not a trend. The nightly snapshot that records queue size, inflow and outflow shipped on 16 September 2026 and is not yet installed. The first measured inflow and outflow pair arrives on the second night after it runs, a four-week average becomes meaningful at about week five, and the saves-versus-reads crossing this plate is really asking about needs roughly eight weeks of nights. Until then the queue line is drawn between the only two observations that exist, and the saves-per-week figure is left blank rather than reconstructed from them.
Read the note

The reading rate more than doubled in the last quarter, from about three articles a week to eight, and the queue still grew. That is the whole argument for measuring inflow separately: a backlog that grows while reading accelerates is a saving problem, not a reading problem, and nothing on this page can yet prove which it is.

The two endpoints are not the same kind of measurement. The 2023 figure is counted from an export, filtered to rows marked saved and not read. The 2026 figure is a live count of everything in the queue status. They agree on definition and differ on instrument, which is the usual reason a long-run series has a seam in it.

What is published. Counts, years, topics and source hosts. No titles, no addresses, no article text, no people, organisations or locations. Redaction happens where the data is built rather than where it is drawn: the payload is assembled field by field out of aggregates, so nothing downstream can print what it was never handed. A scan then walks the whole written tree for every article title and address in the corpus, and refuses to publish on a single match.
